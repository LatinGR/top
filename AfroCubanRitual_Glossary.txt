## Orishas and Sacred Figures

| Term | Description and ritual significance |
|---|---|
| **Ochún / Oshún** | Orisha of rivers, fresh water, beauty, love, fertility, 
sensuality, prosperity, and diplomacy. Often associated with yellow and gold. |
| **Yemayá / Yemojá** | Orisha and mother of the waters, especially the sea. 
Associated with motherhood, protection, maternity, and the mysteries of the ocean. |
| **Changó / Shangó** | Orisha of thunder, lightning, fire, drumming, virility, 
justice, kingship, and masculine power. His ritual dances 
often include energetic movements and gestures representing lightning or swordplay. |
| **Eleguá / Elegguá / Elegbá** | Orisha of crossroads, beginnings, 
communication, and destiny. He opens and closes ritual pathways and is commonly honored first. |
| **Obatalá** | Orisha of wisdom, creation, purity, elderhood, 
and human consciousness. Dances may be slow, dignified, and associated with white clothing. |
| **Oyá / Oiá** | Orisha of winds, storms, transformation, cemeteries, 
and the marketplace. Her dances may imitate wind, swirling cloth, and sudden changes of direction. |
| **Ogún / Oggún** | Orisha of iron, labor, tools, hunting, war, roads, 
and technology. His dances often evoke cutting, clearing, forging, or the work of a blacksmith. |
| **Ochosi / Oshosi** | Orisha of hunting, justice, precision, and the forest. 
His dance may suggest archery and tracking. |
| **Babalu-Ayé / Babalú Ayé** | Orisha associated with illness, healing, 
epidemics, and bodily suffering. His dance often involves a staff, 
covered body, or gestures suggesting pain and recovery. |
| **Orula / Orunmila** | Orisha of divination, wisdom, and destiny. 
He is central to Ifá but is not usually represented 
through the same kind of possession dance as many other orishas. |
| **Olokun** | Powerful deity of the deep ocean, wealth, mystery, 
and hidden knowledge. Ritual representation is often solemn and restrained. |
| **Yewá / Yegguá** | Orisha associated with chastity, death, 
cemeteries, and secluded or liminal spaces. |
| **Aganyú / Agayú** | Orisha of volcanoes, rivers, immense strength, 
and the earth’s power. His dances may be broad, forceful, and expansive. |
| **Inle / Erinle** | Orisha of medicine, fishing, hunting, and healing. |
| **Osain / Ozain** | Orisha of herbs, forests, medicinal plants, and ritual knowledge. |
| **Naná Burukú** | Elder female divinity associated with mud, 
primordial waters, illness, death, and ancestral wisdom. 
Particularly prominent in some West African and Afro-Cuban traditions. |
| **Obá** | Orisha associated with marriage, loyalty, sacrifice, and feminine strength. |
| **Orí** | The spiritual head or personal consciousness and destiny of an individual. |
| **Ibeji / Ibeyis** | Divine twins associated with children, play, protection, luck, 
and overcoming difficulty. |
| **Añá** | Sacred power of the consecrated batá drums; sometimes 
described as a spiritual force or orisha connected with drumming. |
| **Eggún / Egún** | Ancestors or the collective ancestral dead. 
Ancestor reverence is fundamental to many Afro-Cuban rituals. |
| **Echu / Èṣù** | In Yoruba religion, a complex divine messenger 
and trickster figure. In Cuba, his identity and ritual relationship are often closely 
connected with Eleguá, though religious interpretations vary. |

## Key Religious and Ritual Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Regla de Ocha** | The Cuban religious tradition commonly known as Santería or La Regla de Ocha. |
| **Santería** | A widely used name for the Afro-Cuban religion centered on orisha devotion, 
initiation, offerings, divination, music, and ritual possession. |
| **Ocha** | Short form of Regla de Ocha; also refers broadly to the orisha-centered religious system. |
| **Ifá** | Divinatory and philosophical system associated especially with Orula and the babalawo. |
| **Babalawo** | Initiated male priest of Ifá. |
| **Iyálorisha / Iyalosha** | Initiated female priest of an orisha; literally, broadly, 
“mother of the orisha.” |
| **Babalorisha** | Initiated male priest of an orisha. |
| **Santera / Santero** | Common terms for an initiated female or male practitioner, respectively. |
| **Iyawó / Iyawo** | Newly initiated devotee during the first year after initiation. |
| **Olórisha** | A person initiated into devotion to an orisha. |
| **Aborisha** | A devotee who has received certain rites but has not undergone full initiation. |
| **Santo** | “Saint”; in Cuban usage, may refer to an orisha through 
Catholic-orisha correspondences or to the initiation ceremony itself. |
| **Hacer santo** | To undergo initiation into the priesthood or religious service of an orisha. |
| **Asiento** | The initiation ceremony in which an orisha is ritually 
established or “seated” in the initiate. |
| **Kariocha / Kari-Ocha** | Another name for the major initiation into Regla de Ocha. |
| **Itá** | Divinatory reading and set of ritual instructions received after initiation. |
| **Ituto** | Funeral or deconsecration rites performed after the death of an initiate. |
| **Ebbó / Ebó** | Offering, sacrifice, cleansing, 
or prescribed ritual action intended to restore balance or address a divinatory problem. |
| **Rogación de cabeza** | Ritual “feeding” or blessing of the head, 
often involving cool or white substances and intended to restore spiritual balance. |
| **Despojo** | Spiritual cleansing or removal of harmful influences. |
| **Limpieza** | Purification or cleansing ritual. |
| **Omiero** | Sacred liquid prepared from herbs and other ritual substances; 
used for purification, consecration, and initiation. |
| **Aché / Ashé** | Sacred life force, authority, blessing, or spiritual potency. |
| **Maferefun** | Expression of praise, gratitude, or acknowledgment to an orisha, 
often followed by the deity’s name. |
| **Moyuba** | Formal invocation and salute to ancestors, orishas, and religious elders. |
| **Toya** | Ritual or ceremonial language in some Afro-Cuban contexts; usage varies by tradition. |
| **Ilé** | House, religious family, or temple community. |
| **Ilé Ocha** | House or community dedicated to orisha worship. |
| **Padrino / Madrina** | Godfather or godmother in the religious lineage. |
| **Oyugbona** | Senior ritual sponsor or assistant to the godparent during initiation. |
| **Rama** | Religious lineage or branch. |
| **Familia de santo** | Initiatory family connected through godparentage and ritual lineage. |
| **Cabildo** | Afro-Cuban mutual-aid and religious association, 
historically organized by African ethnic or “nation” identities. |
| **Tambor** | Drum ceremony or sacred drumming event; also means drum in Spanish. |
| **Toque** | A ritual drumming performance for an orisha. |
| **Güemilere** | Public or communal celebration honoring the orishas, 
often involving drumming, singing, dancing, and spirit possession. |
| **Bembé** | Celebration involving drumming, singing, dancing, offerings, 
and interaction with the orishas. |
| **Fiesta de santo** | Celebration held for an orisha, often on the deity’s feast day or anniversary. |
| **Velorio** | Wake or vigil, sometimes incorporating religious music and ritual elements. |
| **Misa espiritual** | Spiritual mass or séance-like ceremony 
involving prayers, songs, mediums, and the dead. |
| **Espiritismo de cordón** | Cuban spiritualist practice 
involving rhythmic movement, chanting, trance, and collective spiritual work. |
| **Prenda** | Sacred ritual object or vessel, especially in Palo traditions. |
| **Nganga / Nkisi** | Consecrated vessel or spiritual power object in Palo; 
its exact meaning varies among branches. |
| **Palo** | Afro-Cuban religious complex of Central African, especially Kongo-derived, origin. |
| **Palo Monte** | Broad term for Kongo-derived Cuban religious practices. |
| **Mayombe** | A major branch or style of Palo. |
| **Brillumba** | Another Palo branch or ritual tradition. |
| **Kimbisa** | A Palo tradition with distinctive ritual and historical development. |
| **Arará** | Afro-Cuban tradition derived largely from Ewe-Fon peoples of Dahomey; 
its deities are often called vodún. |
| **Vodún / Vodou** | Sacred beings in Arará and related Ewe-Fon traditions. |
| **Abakuá** | Afro-Cuban mutual-aid and initiatory society of Efik 
and related West African origin, historically associated especially 
with male membership and masked ceremonial performance. |
| **Efi / Efik** | West African cultural source associated with Abakuá traditions. |
| **Ñáñigo** | Historical Cuban term often used for an Abakuá member; 
it can be outdated or derogatory depending on context. |

## Ritual Music and Dance Terms

| Term | Description |
|---|---|
| **Batá** | Set of three double-headed, hourglass-shaped drums sacred to Añá 
and central to many orisha ceremonies. |
| **Iyá** | The largest and leading batá drum. |
| **Itótele** | The middle-sized batá drum. |
| **Okónkolo** | The smallest batá drum. |
| **Aberikulá** | Non-consecrated or secular batá drums, in contrast to drums consecrated to Añá. |
| **Añá drums** | Consecrated batá drums used in formal religious ceremonies. |
| **Akpwón / Apkwón** | Lead singer who directs ritual songs and cues the drummers. |
| **Olú batá** | Master batá drummer. |
| **Canto de santo** | Song performed in honor of an orisha. |
| **Coro** | Chorus that responds to the lead singer. |
| **Canto-respuesta** | Call-and-response structure between lead singer and chorus. |
| **Toque de santo** | Sacred drumming sequence associated with the orishas. |
| **Toque de fundamento** | Ritual drumming involving consecrated instruments or deep religious authority. |
| **Rumba** | Cuban musical and dance tradition with African-derived foundations; 
not identical to Santería, though it shares rhythms, movement, and historical contexts. |
| **Guaguancó** | A rumba form featuring singing, percussion, and partnered movement. |
| **Yambú** | Slower rumba form, traditionally associated with older dancers and restrained movement. |
| **Columbia** | Fast, often solo-oriented rumba form emphasizing virtuosity and rhythmic dialogue. |
| **Conga** | Cuban percussion and dance form with African-derived roots; 
may appear in secular or ceremonial contexts. |
| **Maraca** | Rattle used to accompany singing and ritual music. |
| **Chékere / Agbé** | Gourd rattle covered with a net of beads or shells. |
| **Chekeré** | Alternate spelling of chékere. |
| **Caja** | “Box” or lead drum in some Cuban percussion contexts; meaning varies by genre. |
| **Quinto** | Small, high-pitched drum that improvises in rumba and related percussion ensembles. |
| **Salidor** | Larger supporting drum in some rumba ensembles. |
| **Tres-dos** | Rhythmic pattern or supporting drum role in rumba. |
| **Clave** | Two wooden sticks and, more broadly, the organizing rhythmic pattern of much Cuban music. |
| **Guataca** | Metal hoe blade or similar percussion instrument used in some Afro-Cuban ensembles. |
| **Cata** | Wooden percussion stick or block used in certain ensembles. |
| **Shekere dance** | Dance accompanied by the shekere; not necessarily 
a distinct religious ritual but common in Afro-Cuban performance. |
| **Ritual possession** | State in which a devotee is understood 
to be mounted or entered by an orisha or spirit. |
| **Montar el santo** | Literally, “the orisha mounts”; 
refers to possession or embodiment of an orisha. |
| **Subir el santo** | To cause or facilitate the appearance of an orisha during ceremony. |
| **Bailar para el santo** | To dance for or in honor of an orisha. |
| **Bailar al santo** | To dance before or for the orisha. |
| **Danza de posesión** | Possession dance in which movement, 
gesture, costume, and rhythm identify a deity. |
| **Trance** | Altered state associated with spiritual manifestation or possession. |
| **Yubá** | A term associated with certain Afro-Cuban rhythmic and dance forms; local meanings vary. |
| **Makuta** | Afro-Cuban dance and percussion tradition 
with strong Central African roots, especially associated with Arará and other communities. |
| **Bembé rhythm** | Rhythmic and ceremonial context 
associated with bembé celebrations; specific patterns vary. |
| **Iyesá** | Afro-Cuban musical and religious tradition 
associated with Yoruba-derived communities, especially in Matanzas and other areas. |
| **Dahomean dance** | Dance connected 
with Arará ritual traditions and Ewe-Fon-derived deities. |
| **Kongo dance** | Broad label for Central African-derived movement and ritual forms 
associated with Palo and related traditions. |

## Terms Concerning Specific Orisha Dances

- **Ochún dance**
  - Hip circles and flowing, sensual movements
  - Gestures associated with mirrors, jewelry, rivers, and attracting beauty
  - Yellow, gold, fans, and handkerchiefs may be used symbolically

- **Yemayá dance**
  - Wave-like arm and torso movements
  - Gestures suggesting the sea, waves, sailing, or motherhood
  - Blue and white are common ritual colors

- **Changó dance**
  - Strong, vigorous movements
  - Gestures associated with thunder, lightning, fire, weapons, and virility
  - Red and white are commonly associated with Changó

- **Eleguá dance**
  - Quick, playful, mischievous, and liminal movement
  - Gestures may suggest opening roads, carrying a messenger’s staff, or moving between crossroads

- **Oyá dance**
  - Fast turns, sweeping arms, windlike gestures, and sudden changes
  - Often associated with her whip, winds, storms, and the cemetery

- **Ogún dance**
  - Forceful, grounded movement
  - Gestures resembling cutting through brush, forging iron, hunting, or wielding tools

- **Obatalá dance**
  - Slow, controlled, dignified movement
  - Emphasis on age, wisdom, purity, and calm

- **Ochosi dance**
  - Hunting and archery gestures
  - Light, precise, alert movement

- **Babalu-Ayé dance**
  - Bent posture, shaking, careful walking, and gestures suggesting illness or healing
  - May involve a staff or symbolic covering
